One iteration, one warp: where is the straggler?

Scope reduced to a single dispatch warp's token loop: which phase of the iteration is slow, and which warp exactly is last, from the existing trace.

E7 follow-up · rank 1, repeat 0 of results/ratio1_smid.pt · 2026-10-13

Scope reduced to one dispatch (rank 1, GPU 5, uniform routing, 4,096 tokens, 16 SMs), one warp, one iteration of the token loop. Two questions: which phase of the iteration is the problematic one, and which warp exactly is the straggler. Both are answered from the per-token stamps already in the trace; nothing was re-run.

1. The iteration and its phases

Each dispatch warp runs the same loop for each of its tokens: wait for the previous token's stores to be accepted (C1, tma_store_wait), prepare the token (C2a: top-k loads and the destination-slot atomic issued; C2b: the atomic's return and the TMA load of the token landing in shared memory), then issue one bulk store per destination (C3). The trace stamps each boundary per token, and per lane the store issue and acceptance. A warp's finish is the acceptance of its last store.

2. Which phase

C3, the store issue, in the first iterations. The straggler spends 354 µs of its 592 µs in C3 against 105 µs for the fastest warp; C1 is 113 vs 53 µs and C2 122 vs 75 µs. The excess is front-loaded: the straggler's first six iterations spend 253 µs in C3 and the remaining twelve 101 µs; for the fastest warp the numbers are 37 and 68 µs. Across all 240 warps the median first-six C3 is 105 µs and the median rest 114 µs. The correlation of a warp's finish with its total time per phase is 0.94 for C3, 0.89 for C1, 0.71 for C2a and 0.71 for C2b.

So within one iteration the problematic phase is the issue of the bulk stores, not the wait, not the load and not the atomic. The issue instruction blocks when the per-lane queue of about seven stores is full (E1); at the start of the kernel every warp fills that queue at once, and the queue of a slow TPC drains slowest, so its warps pay tens of µs per iteration until the burst clears, and never catch up.

3. Which warp

The exact straggler is block 2, dispatch warp 0, on physical SM 128, finishing at 592 µs with 18 tokens and 67 stores. It is not an anomaly but the predictable product of three structural facts. (1) Its block runs on a slow TPC (physical SM 128, paired with SM 129, whose block finishes at the same level), and its fifteen warps all finish within about 10 µs of each other except three. (2) Those three, dispatch warps 3, 7 and 11, sit on warp scheduler 3 together with a notify warp that finishes early, so they share their scheduler with two other dispatch warps instead of three and finish 62 µs before their block (Figure 3); the straggler is on scheduler 0. (3) It is dispatch warp 0, and the static token assignment gives warp 0 of every block an 18th token, 14 KB more work than its neighbours.

4. What this says about the method

  1. Granularity is sufficient. One iteration of one warp is decomposed into four phases with 32 ns stamps at about 1 % cost; the straggler is identified by name (block, warp, physical SM, scheduler) and its excess is placed in one phase and one range of iterations.
  2. The straggler is structural, at three levels. TPC (placement, E7), warp scheduler share within the SM (the notify warps' placement), and the token assignment's remainder. Each is a property of the launch, not of the fabric, and each is predictable before the kernel runs once placement is known.
  3. The remedy is local. Stagger the first issues to avoid the start burst, assign the remainder tokens to scheduler-3 warps, give slow TPCs fewer tokens: all of them software, all testable with the same trace.
  4. What is still not visible is where the slow TPC's bytes wait after they leave the SM. The phase is known (C3), the location is known to the TPC, the cause beyond that is the dark segment.
