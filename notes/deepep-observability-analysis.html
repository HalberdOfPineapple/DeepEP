DeepEP on the Wire: Degradations, Blind Spots and the Experiments That Would Expose Them

DeepEP dispatch is the most demanding memory-semantic workload a single GPU node runs today: 240 warps per GPU issuing 14 KB bulk stores into seven peers' memories, a control plane of 8-byte stores and atomics, two cross-GPU barriers, and a CPU spinning on a mailbox, all inside one cooperative kernel that is meant to overlap with GEMMs. This page asks what can go wrong on the fabric while that runs, what each problem looks like from today's tools, why they stop there, and what measurement would get to the cause.

Nothing on this page was measured yet; every number is derived from the code or quoted from the paper, and every claim about hardware behaviour is a hypothesis with its experiment next to it.

  • C2 answered, P2 withdrawn as written. cp.async.bulk.wait_group returns on local acceptance (150–400 ns, the same for a local and a peer destination); only a system-scope release waits for the peer (~1.8 µs ≈ the 1.2–1.4 µs round trip). The window is the per-lane async-proxy queue (~7 stores, drained at ~50 GB/s per SM), and back-pressure lands on the cp.async.bulk instruction itself. Dispatch throughput is min(~33 GB/s per SM remote, ~285 GB/s per GPU) at four ranks; half of a warp's 17 µs iteration is store-issue stall on an idle fabric. Figure P2 and the "window closes at 45 µs" argument are superseded by the E0 report.
  • P1 sharpened. Under incast the hot rank's intake caps near 320 GB/s and every sender's per-destination latency is identical for hot and idle destinations: the per-SM store FIFO is shared across destinations, so a receiver bottleneck slows all of a sender's traffic uniformly and idle destinations' stores stretch over the whole kernel. The wait_group.read variant changes nothing. Figure P1's panel A is cheap; its panel B happens upstream of the link, in the SM.
  • P3 downgraded at four ranks: slot atomics complete in 96 ns whether or not the counters share a line.
  • P4 measured, and it is the incast case that matters. An 8-byte count store crosses in ~1.8 µs on a quiet fabric; with a balanced data plane its p50 stays at 2.3 µs while the p99 reaches 18 µs; under incast (ratio 4) the p50 is 31 µs and the p99 70 µs, and the receiver's wait for all peers' counts grows to 33 µs, because SM 0's count stores sit in the same per-SM FIFO as the dispatch warps' data stores to the hot rank. Relocating the receive array by 4 MiB changes nothing: the address does not select a path.
  • Clock synchronisation corrected. A ping-pong calibration closes triangles to ~100 ns, but the GPUs' %globaltimer clocks drift by milliseconds per hour relative to each other; calibration must bracket each measured kernel (≈12 ms for four ranks), not "once per session" as §4.2 says.
  • Instrumentation validated: per-lane trace at 0.1–1 % overhead through an existing kernel argument, no shared memory used. A warp-wide ballot inside a lane branch deadlocks the kernel; see common/README.md.

After E5–E9 (2026-10-13, GPUs 4–7): E6: a delayed rank costs its peers the delay minus an 85 µs launch allowance, and the aligned stamps name the rank; host stamps separate a busy GPU from a slow host. E5: HBM-bound neighbours stretch the token load ×1.6 and the store path ×1.3 on the loaded sender, a loaded receiver does not slow stores into it, and the cooperative launch waits 1–2 ms behind even short background kernels. E7: the warp tail is set by the physical TPC each block lands on (fixed levels 1.0 : 1.2 : 1.4 per GPU, R² 88 %), not by the warps' work (R² 1 %). E8: a concurrent combine costs under 10 %, a concurrent NCCL all-to-all doubles the data plane with the same stamp signature as self-saturation; an NVML counter read before launch perturbs the kernel by 20 %. E9: the 128-token decode call is 142 µs, of which the kernel is 43 and the host path 100. Reports: experiments/observability-exploration/h100x4/.

The frame: target, phases, attribution

Read this section first. Everything below it (the nine problems, the ten challenges, the experiments) is an instance of one of the cells in the table at the end of this section.

The target

For every dispatch or combine that ran slower than it should have, say which phase absorbed the extra time, at which point along the path, and from which cause, using signals cheap enough to stay on in production. The output is a per-kernel time decomposition with a cause attached to each excess. "Observability" here means the set of signals plus the rules that turn them into that decomposition. The research question is where that decomposition is impossible with the signals that exist, and what minimal new signal (a hardware counter, a library hook, a protocol change) would close each hole.

Three attributions are wanted, in increasing order of difficulty:

  1. Phase: which of the six phases below the time went to. Answerable on one rank with in-kernel timestamps.
  2. Location: for the transfer phase, where along sender SM → sender egress → switch → receiver ingress → receiver memory the bytes waited. Partly answerable from the sender, dark in the middle.
  3. Cause: whether the excess came from this kernel's own routing (incast), from its own SM/link ratio, from a peer that was late, from compute running beside it, or from another job on the same links. Needs the phase decomposition on every rank on one clock, plus counters outside the kernel.

So the direct answer to "can we attribute a symptom to a phase": on one rank, yes, for A, B, C, E and F, with in-kernel stamps costing about 1 % (built and used in E0–E9, including the host path in E6 and E9). Across ranks, yes for A, B and E once the clocks are aligned around each kernel (E4 showed how, and that a one-off calibration is not enough; E6 recovers an injected delay 1:1). Inside D, mostly no: the sender's visibility ends when its SM's queue accepts the store, and the receiver has no per-store event at all. The one exception found so far is at the very start of D: the drain rate of the sender's own TPC, which E7 showed to be a fixed per-TPC property that sets the whole warp tail. What a sender can say about D is only "my queue towards the link was draining slowly", which cannot distinguish its own egress, the switch, the peer's ingress, or a foreign job. That is the hole every "problem" in section 2 that involves the fabric (P1, P7, P9) falls into, and the one the tooling research has to fill with something outside the kernel: a per-destination completion counter in the memory system, timestamped port counters, or a receiver-side arrival event.

The three mechanisms of degradation, in this frame. Everything in section 2 is one of: (i) more time in C1/C3 because the SM's queue drains slower (the link is saturated, by this kernel's own fan-out, by incast on one destination, by a sibling kernel or by a foreign job); (ii) more time in A/E because some rank was later than the others (compute skew, a slower peer, a long tail); (iii) more time in B because small control stores share the queue with the data. Mechanism (i) is visible as a symptom but not attributable to a location; (ii) is attributable with aligned clocks; (iii) is measurable with two stamps and aligned clocks.

0. The short version

  • DeepEP has no data-plane "packet" and no receiver-side arrival event at all. Tokens are moved by cp.async.bulk stores from shared memory straight into a peer's buffer, with no flag, no fence, and no poll on the receiving side. The receiver learns that data exists only at the closing barrier, hundreds of microseconds later. FabricPerf's T1 to T5 cannot be attached anywhere on this path. The only per-token event that exists is on the sender: the cp.async.bulk.wait_group each lane executes before its warp reuses its staging slot.
  • That wait is not a round trip, and the real limit is the SM's store queue (measured in E1 and E0; the original claim here, "one token per warp per round trip", is withdrawn). wait_group returns once the SM's copy engine has accepted the store, a few hundred ns, whether the destination is local or a peer. A warp is held back by the per-lane queue of about seven bulk stores, drained at about 50 GB/s per SM; back-pressure from a saturated link shows first as a stall on the store instruction and then as longer slot waits. Throughput is min(per-SM drain × SMs, about 285 GB/s per GPU at four ranks).
  • The good news hidden in the code: stores and waits are per lane, and lane k talks to exactly one destination. Timestamping around each lane's wait yields issue-to-completion latency per (source, destination, token, bytes) on a single clock, with no second GPU, no clock sync and no extra traffic. It is the memory-semantic analogue of a NIC completion queue, and it is the one sensor this workload gives for free, provided the semantics of "complete" for a remote bulk store are first calibrated.
  • Contention in DeepEP is decided by addresses, not channels: eight slot counters in one 32-byte sector receive about 21,000 returning atomics per dispatch per GPU; every rank's barrier adds land in one 16-byte word of the receiver; 264 control-plane stores per rank pair compete for the same links as 270 MB of bulk data. None of these is visible as more than "the kernel took longer".
  • Local symptom, remote cause. Two grid-wide syncs and two NVLink barriers per dispatch mean that a slow destination, a late launch, or a straggling warp on one rank appears on every other rank as barrier time. Attributing it needs a cross-rank timeline. On one node the GPU clocks are stable enough to build one with a single calibration; across nodes, with GIN, they are not.
  • Nine problems, nine experiments. Section 2 gives mechanism, symptom, dead end, missing information and validation for each. Section 4 orders them into a programme that runs on this 8×H100 node with a small instrumentation patch and the generators DeepEP already ships, including the unbalanced-gate generator that makes incast reproducible.

1.1 The per-warp pipeline, and where its time can go

Three structural facts drive everything below.

  1. The window is one token per warp. tma_store_wait() is cp.async.bulk.wait_group 0, not the .read variant, so the warp waits for full completion of all of its outstanding stores, not merely for the staging slot to be free. In flight per GPU: at most 240 warps × 5.29 destinations × 14,464 B ≈ 18 MB. At 400 GB/s that is 45 µs of bandwidth-delay product, comfortable against a quiet-fabric latency of a few µs, and not comfortable once completion latency at some destination grows past tens of µs.
  2. Each iteration takes ≈ 40 µs on a saturated fabric (0.68 ms per dispatch ÷ 17 iterations), against single-digit µs of nominal latency. The warp therefore spends most of its life inside wait_group, and that waiting time is exactly the queueing delay of its stores. FabricPerf laments that the NIC queue-depth signal disappears in scale-up fabrics; here it reappears as the per-lane wait duration.
  3. Lane k owns destination k. The stores at dispatch.cuh:373 are issued by whichever lane holds a non-null dst_ptr, each lane commits its own bulk group, and each lane executes its own wait_group before __syncwarp. Per-destination completion is already separated by the hardware; only the warp-level __syncwarp merges it back.

2. Degradation catalogue

Each entry answers the same five questions: what mechanically goes wrong, what you would see with the tools available today (nsys/Kineto kernel timelines, DeepEP's own bench_kineto GB/s numbers, NVML and CUPTI counters, Nsight Compute), where those tools stop, what information would resolve it, and the experiment that confirms or refutes the mechanism. Severity tags are my estimate of how likely the mechanism is to matter in production, not measured impact.

P1 · Destination incast and victim flows likely, large

Mechanism

Expert routing is never uniform. A rank hosting popular experts receives more tokens; DeepEP's test generator (deep_ep.utils.gate.get_unbalanced_scores, parameter ratio) models exactly this, with one rank receiving ratio× the others. That rank's NVLink ingress (18 links, ≈ 450 GB/s one direction) and its L2/HBM write path become the bottleneck while seven senders keep pushing. Inherent cost: the dispatch cannot finish before the hot rank has absorbed its bytes.

The non-inherent cost is what matters for observability. Each sending warp waits for all of its lanes' stores. A warp whose token touches the hot rank, which under skew is most warps, holds its staging slot until the hot store completes, and so stops sending to the six idle ranks too (warp-level head-of-line blocking). Separately, if the sender's NVLink egress credits or the switch egress queues are shared between destinations, stores to idle ranks are physically delayed behind hot-rank stores (fabric-level head-of-line blocking, the "victim flow" of data-centre incast). The first is a DeepEP design fact; the second is an unmeasured property of NVLink/NVSwitch.

Symptom today

Dispatch kernel time rises on every rank equally (the closing barrier equalises it); bench_kineto reports lower GB/s; cumulative_local_expert_recv_stats shows the imbalance after the fact; NVML per-link counters show the hot GPU's RX near line rate and others below. Nothing says whether the idle ranks' ingress was idle because nothing was sent to them or because their senders were stuck.

Dead end

Per-GPU and per-link byte counters cannot separate "stalled behind a slow destination" from "nothing to send". Nsight Compute cannot replay a cooperative multi-GPU kernel (its barriers wait for peers that do not replay). Kineto sees one kernel per rank. There is no per-(source, destination) latency anywhere in the stack.

Missing

Per-destination completion latency as seen by senders over time, which separates the two kinds of head-of-line blocking: under pure warp-level blocking, stores to idle ranks complete as fast as in the balanced run while the warp waits for the hot one; under fabric-level blocking, their completion latency rises too. Plus receiver-side ingress rate per source, which software cannot provide (only NVSwitch port counters know source and destination).

Experiment E2

Instrument each lane's store issue and wait_group return (Section 4.2). Sweep ratio ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} with the precise generator. Record per-(src, dst) latency distributions per 50 µs bin and per-warp stall time. Prediction if fabric-level blocking exists: p50 latency to idle ranks grows with ratio; if only warp-level: it does not, but warp stall time and idle-rank ingress gaps do. Second manipulation: a patched kernel that uses wait_group.read in the loop and a full wait only before the barrier (slot free as soon as the source bytes are read). If throughput to idle ranks recovers, the warp-level mechanism is confirmed and quantified; if completion latency to idle ranks remains inflated, the fabric shares queues across destinations.

P2 · Throughput coupled to latency through the one-token window likely under contention

Mechanism

With 240 warps and one token in flight each, sender throughput is bounded by in-flight bytes / completion latency. On a quiet fabric the 18 MB window is ample. Under any of P1, P6 or P7 the completion latency grows, and once it exceeds roughly 45 µs the window, not the links, limits throughput: the fabric drains, latency drops, warps resume, the fabric fills, a sawtooth. There is no adaptive credit: the window is fixed at compile time by shared-memory capacity (15 slots of 14,496 B in 227 KB).

A second, more basic unknown sits underneath: what event does cp.async.bulk.wait_group actually wait for when the destination is a peer GPU? Completion of a bulk store means the writes are "performed", but whether that is acceptance by the local memory system or acknowledgement from the peer's L2 decides whether the measured wait is a queueing signal or a round trip. The closing barrier's correctness relies on wait_group followed by red.release.sys, so at least the pair makes remote visibility certain; which half carries the latency is unknown.

Symptom today

Measured GB/s falls below the per-GPU link rate with no counter saturated: NVML link utilisation is well under 100%, HBM bandwidth is low, SM issue slots are free. Classic "nothing is busy but it is slow".

Dead end

No profiler exposes outstanding bulk-store bytes or async-proxy wait reasons. PC sampling attributes the stall to the wait_group instruction and stops. Nothing relates that stall to a destination or a latency.

Missing

Calibrated semantics of bulk-store completion against destination and size; in-flight bytes per destination over time (sum of issued minus completed, computable from the lane trace); the hardware's own credit limit on outstanding remote stores, measured by issuing without waiting.

Experiments E0, E1

E1 (calibration). A standalone kernel: one warp stores B bytes from shared memory to peer p (via the same ncclGetLsaPointer window), timestamps around wait_group; sweep B from 16 B to 64 KB, p over self and 7 peers, and compare against three references: a wait_group.read wait, a fence.acq_rel.sys after the store, and a ping-pong round trip through the same buffer. The gaps between those curves say what "complete" includes. Also issue N stores back to back without waiting and time the N-th issue: the knee is the hardware's outstanding-store limit per SM, the credit that DeepEP never sees. E0 (window). Run the unmodified dispatch with num_sms ∈ {2,4,8,16,24,32} and tokens ∈ {256, 1024, 4096}; plot GB/s against in-flight bytes. The slope in the linear region is 1/latency; where it flattens the links are the limit. Repeat under P6 load to show the knee moving.

P3 · Address hot spots: the slot-counter sector and the barrier word certain mechanism, size unknown

Mechanism

scaleup_atomic_sender_count is eight consecutive ints at one offset of the window (workspace.cuh:17): one 32-byte sector in one 128-byte line in one L2 slice. Every (token, destination) pair on the GPU does a returning atomicAdd on it: ≈ 21,700 per dispatch, from 240 warps, all serialised at that slice. The atomic's return value is on the critical path of the warp (the destination address depends on it), so its latency, not just its throughput, adds to every iteration. Unlike a bandwidth problem this one does not scale down with fewer tokens per warp: in the decode regime (P8) it is a fixed cost per token.

The barrier uses the same shape in the other direction: every rank's SM 0 performs red.release.sys.add into the same 4-byte word on each peer. Eight system-scope releases converging on one line per rank per barrier, twice per dispatch, and the release semantics force each issuing SM to drain its prior stores first. The notify receive array is 264 adjacent 8-byte slots, written by eight peers, polled by 128 threads: a third address-concentrated region.

Symptom today

None that is specific. Nsight Compute, if it could run, would report L2 atomic throughput for the whole kernel, dwarfed by 300 MB of data traffic. Per-iteration time looks slightly long. In decode, dispatch latency has an unexplained floor.

Dead end

No per-address or per-slice contention counter is exposed; L2 metrics are device-wide totals; and the kernel cannot be replayed under ncu anyway. The only software-visible evidence is the atomic's return latency, which no tool records.

Missing

Per-line serialisation (queue) depth at the L2 atomic unit, or its software proxy: return latency of each atomic against the number of concurrent issuers; the address-to-slice mapping so that layout changes can be reasoned about rather than guessed.

Experiment E3

Timestamp around the atomicAdd in the issuing lane (two %globaltimer reads, or clock64 for 1 ns resolution). Baseline versus a layout patch that pads each counter to its own 128-byte line, versus warp-aggregated allocation (one atomic per warp per destination, which the deduplication already enables). Vary tokens and num_sms. Expected: baseline latency grows with concurrent warps and shows a long tail; padding removes the cross-destination serialisation but not the per-destination one; the kernel-level effect is small in prefill and measurable in decode. For the barrier, measure arrive-to-release time on each rank with and without a padded, per-sender signal layout (eight words instead of one, polled together). The difference is the cost of the incast word.

P4 · Control plane queued behind the data plane plausible, regime-dependent

Mechanism

The 264 count stores from SM 0 leave through the same SM memory pipeline, the same L2, the same NVLink ports and the same switch queues as the bulk stores of the 15 dispatch warps on that SM and the 225 on the others. They are 8-byte system-scope relaxed stores with no priority. If the fabric queues are deep with 14 KB stores, a count store that nominally takes 2 µs may take as long as the queue ahead of it. The receiver's phase F cannot finish until the last of eight senders' counts arrive, and the CPU allocation (torch.empty of a ≈ 500 MB output) cannot start until phase H. In prefill this is hidden behind 0.7 ms of data; in decode, or when the hot rank's queues are deep (P1), the control plane can become the critical path, and the copy epilogue starts late.

The same applies to the opening barrier's atomics when a previous kernel's stores are still draining, and to the hybrid kernels' per-channel tail signals, which are issued every six tokens and polled by the forwarding warps.

Symptom today

A gap between the dispatch kernel's end and the copy epilogue's start on the Kineto timeline, or a CPU thread shown spinning in current_stream_wait. It is indistinguishable from allocator time, PCIe mailbox latency or launch overhead.

Dead end

No tool distinguishes control stores from data stores: both are "NVLink TX bytes". The receiver poll's resolution is one L2 round trip and it only records that the count eventually arrived.

Missing

One-way latency of small stores against concurrent bulk load on the same source-destination pair, and whether it depends on address (does a different offset in the window take a different link?). This is the clearest case where a cross-GPU timeline is required: the event is a store on A and a poll success on B. On one node the %globaltimer offsets between GPUs are stable (FabricPerf: 0.1 ns per run drift intra-node), so a single ping-pong calibration per session is enough.

Experiment E4

Timestamp phase E issue on the sender and phase F success per slot on the receiver (both in SM 0, negligible perturbation). Run (a) notify alone, by launching with num_tokens small enough that the data plane is trivial; (b) full dispatch; (c) full dispatch with ratio = 4. Expected: (a) gives the quiet-fabric one-way time; (b) and (c) inflate the tail by the data queueing. Address probe: relocate the receive array by a few MB (the EPSignals struct has 15 MiB of unused hybrid fields) and repeat; a change in latency distribution reveals that address selects a path.

P5 · Skew amplification: barriers, grid syncs and straggler warps certain, size depends on the pipeline

Mechanism

Three synchronisations gate every dispatch: the opening NVLink barrier (no rank writes until all eight have entered), the closing grid sync (no SM of a rank proceeds until its slowest warp has completed its last store), and the closing NVLink barrier (no rank proceeds until the slowest rank's slowest warp has). Launch skew between ranks, caused by CPU scheduling, differing previous kernels or the Python side, is converted directly into idle fabric at ①. Per-warp work varies with the destination mix of its ≈ 17 tokens and with each destination's state, so warps finish at different times and the fabric drains in a tail at ③, FabricPerf's channel-imbalance observation in a different costume. In the hybrid kernel the forwarding warps add a third kind of wait: polling eight scale-out tails for data that may arrive in bursts.

Symptom today

Kernel durations that differ per rank on the Kineto timeline by exactly the launch skew; a bandwidth-versus-time curve (if one could be drawn) that ramps down instead of ending sharply; "dispatch is slow" with every rank blaming the others.

Dead end

Kineto's cross-process clocks are aligned to a few µs at best and show kernel boundaries only. Inside the kernel, no tool records when the barrier released, when each warp finished, or in which order peers arrived. The barrier itself destroys the information: a counter reaching 8 says nothing about who was last.

Missing

A cross-rank in-kernel timeline: per rank, timestamps of entry, barrier arrive, barrier release, notify phases, last store completion per warp, closing barrier arrive and release. Peer arrival order at a barrier can be recovered without clocks by replacing the red.add with a fetch-add whose return value is the arrival index, at the cost of one round trip per rank per barrier, acceptable for diagnosis.

Experiment E6, E7

E6. Record the timeline above; inject known skew with a CPU sleep on one rank before launch (0, 20, 100, 500 µs); verify that fabric-idle time at ① equals the injected skew and that no rank's data plane starts earlier. E7. From the per-warp last-completion timestamps compute the tail fraction (time between first and last warp completion over total) under uniform and skewed routing; correlate each warp's completion time with its tokens' destination counts. If destination mix explains most variance, the fix is scheduling; if not, the fabric path does, which points back to P1 and P9.

P6 · Interference with concurrent compute the stated use case, unmeasured

Mechanism

DeepEP runs on 16 SMs so that the other 116 can run GEMMs on the compute stream. Both share the L2 (50 MB), the crossbar and the HBM. The sender's bulk loads of x compete with the GEMM for HBM reads; the receiver's incoming 14 KB rows are written into its L2 and evicted to HBM (the 452 MiB buffer never fits), competing with the GEMM's traffic in the opposite direction. Interference is bidirectional: the GEMM slows the communication, and 400 GB/s of ingress plus the eventual copy epilogue (another read and write of every row) slows the GEMM. DeepEP's bandwidth model (get_theoretical_num_sms) picks 16 SMs assuming per-SM write bandwidth of 45 GB/s; under contention that constant is wrong and so is the SM count. FabricPerf's LLC finding (buffers evicted to ≈ 0 % hit rate) is the single-kernel version of this; the two-kernel version is what production runs.

Symptom today

Both kernels are longer when overlapped than when alone, and the sum is less than the serial time, so overlap "works" and nobody looks further. Which side pays, and through which resource, is unknown.

Dead end

Nsight Compute serialises kernels; it cannot measure interference by construction. CUPTI PM sampling can read L2 and HBM counters on a timeline while both run, but they are device totals: 16 SMs of communication and 116 of GEMM are indistinguishable in a DRAM-bandwidth counter. PC sampling attributes stalls per kernel but not to the competing kernel's traffic.

Missing

Per-kernel (per SM-set) attribution of shared memory-system resources, which hardware does not provide, and the software substitute: the communication kernel's own per-lane latency trace (load completion, store completion, atomic return) time-correlated with device-wide counters and with the GEMM's phase. That turns "the device was busy" into "the sender's loads slowed by 2×, the stores did not", which is attribution enough to act on.

Experiment E5

Co-run a large BF16 GEMM on the compute stream at four intensities (none, 25 %, 50 %, 100 % of HBM bandwidth, set by shape), on (a) all ranks, (b) only receivers of a skewed routing, (c) only senders. Measure dispatch GB/s, the per-lane trace (load latency, store latency per destination, atomic latency), GEMM TFLOP/s, and CUPTI PM-sampled DRAM and L2 throughput at 10 µs. Expected signatures: (c) inflates load latency only; (b) inflates store completion latency to the loaded ranks only; both shrink effective window (P2). Repeat with the receive buffer in a size that fits L2 (small num_max_tokens_per_rank) to test whether L2 residency of landing rows changes the GEMM's slowdown.

P7 · Concurrent communication kernels on the same links common in pipelined MoE

Mechanism

Two-microbatch overlap (DualPipe-style) runs dispatch of microbatch i+1 while combine of microbatch i is in flight, on different streams and different SMs. Both are bulk stores from every GPU to every GPU, so both directions of every link carry both kernels. Add a second tenant (another job on the same node, as on this machine right now, or a different EP group) and three store streams share each port. Each kernel sees lower throughput; each has its own window (P2) and its own barriers (P5), so the two can lock-step: one kernel's barrier waits for its slowest warp whose stores are queued behind the other kernel's burst.

Symptom today

Both kernels slower; NVML shows the links busy. No tool says which kernel's bytes occupied which link when, because a link counter counts bytes, not kernels, and the kernels are on the same GPU.

Dead end

Link counters are per GPU per link; there is no per-stream, per-kernel or per-SM NVLink accounting in hardware. Kineto shows two overlapping bars.

Missing

Per-kernel issue accounting (bytes issued per destination per µs, which each kernel can log for itself because it knows the destination) plus the per-lane completion latencies; cross-correlating the two kernels' logs gives who waited behind whom. The hardware-only answer, per-link queue occupancy sampled at µs rate, would make the inference unnecessary.

Experiment E8

Dispatch alone, combine alone, then overlapped on two streams with the traced build of both kernels; compare each kernel's per-destination latency histograms alone versus overlapped; compute the throughput loss and attribute it by which kernel's wait times grew during the other's bursts. Repeat with an unrelated NVLink load from another process (a simple all-to-all) to model a second tenant.

P8 · Decode: the latency regime different problem, same kernel

Mechanism

With 64 to 256 tokens per rank, most warps handle one token or none, there is no steady state and no bandwidth to speak of. The dispatch time is a sum of latencies: launch, opening barrier (8 atomics + poll), notify (local reduction, 264 remote stores, 264 remote polls), one load, one atomic, up to eight stores and their completion, grid sync, closing barrier, PDL trigger, and on the host a PCIe mailbox read, a torch.empty and a launch. Everything in P3, P4 and P5 is a fixed cost here, and the whole kernel is tens of µs, shorter than FabricPerf's per-kernel GPTP handshake (5 to 10 µs) and comparable to its inter-kernel clock jitter. CUDA-graph decoding (do_cpu_sync=False) removes the host but not the device-side chain.

Symptom today

A dispatch that "should" take 10 µs takes 40; the Kineto trace shows one kernel bar and one gap.

Dead end

Nothing resolves inside the bar. Any probing scheme with hundreds of ns of overhead per event changes the answer. Clock synchronisation has to be amortised across many kernels, not performed per kernel.

Missing

A per-dispatch critical-path decomposition across eight GPUs and a CPU with ≈ 100 ns resolution and ≈ 20 ns per event cost: the same timeline as P5 plus host timestamps, with the clock offsets calibrated once and checked for drift, not re-derived per kernel.

Experiment E9

Run the traced build with 128 tokens per rank for 1,000 iterations; build the per-iteration critical path (which rank's which phase was last at each sync edge); report the distribution of each segment. Then apply the P3 padding patch and a variant with the host mailbox replaced by do_cpu_sync=False, and show which segments move. This is where observability pays off directly in µs.

P9 · Links, paths, replays, and the GIN side needs hardware access or the other node

Mechanism

NVLink is lossless through CRC and replay; a degraded lane or a replaying link shows up as a slower path for whichever addresses hash onto it, that is, as unexplained tails in P1 and P7. Across the switch, which of the four chips a flow traverses and whether two ranks' flows share a switch port depends on link assignment that the GPU never reveals. The multi-node hybrid kernels add the GIN path: a store-and-forward pipeline (scale-out send warps → NIC put → forwarding warps poll per-channel tails every six tokens and re-send over NVLink), QP sharing between channels, signal atomics for tails and barriers, and a second clock domain per node. There the fence-free, flag-free data plane is replaced by one with explicit signals, so arrival is observable, but at the price of a far longer chain and the clock-sync problem FabricPerf measured at 36 µs of inter-kernel jitter.

Symptom today

Replay and recovery counters in NVML (per link, cumulative); otherwise tails. In hybrid runs: DeepEP timeout messages naming a channel and a tail value when things go badly wrong, nothing before that.

Dead end

No per-kernel or per-destination association for link-health counters; no NVSwitch port telemetry for users; GIN exposes completion counters per QP but no latency.

Missing

Address-to-link mapping (recoverable by an address sweep correlated with per-link NVML throughput, as the gaps note proposes); per-kernel snapshots of replay counters; for GIN, per-channel put-to-tail-visible latency, which the forwarding warps' polls already expose on the receiving side if timestamped.

Experiment

On this node: NVML replay/recovery counters sampled before and after each dispatch in E2 and E8, to exclude link health as the cause of tails; an address sweep micro-benchmark to map window offsets to links. Hybrid experiments need the second node and are listed as follow-ups in Section 4.

3. Observability challenges this workload exposes

Stated so that each is a property of memory-semantic fabrics, with DeepEP as the witness. Cross-references point to the gap numbers of the companion note.

C1Arrival without an event
The data plane ends at the peer's L2 with no flag, no fence and no poll. The receiver's first knowledge of any token is the closing barrier. FabricPerf's T5 does not exist; any receiver-side measurement is necessarily a hardware ingress counter. (Gaps 2, 11)
C2Completion is the only sensor, and its meaning is undefined
The sender's wait_group is the one per-token event. Whether it reports local acceptance or remote acknowledgement is not documented for remote destinations, and the answer decides whether its duration is a queue depth or a round trip. A tool must calibrate before it measures. (Gap 3)
C3One send, N destinations, one wait
A token is up to eight bulk stores from one shared-memory buffer, completed per lane but waited for per warp. The unit of transmission, the unit of completion and the unit of flow control are three different things. (Gaps 1, 9)
C4Flow control is a software window with no feedback
No credits, no receiver readiness beyond the opening barrier, a fixed window set by shared-memory size. Congestion appears only as time inside wait_group. The hardware's own limit on outstanding remote stores is unknown. (Gap 3)
C5Contention is addressed, not channelled
One 32-byte sector of counters, one barrier word per rank, one 2 KB count array per rank; and buffer rows whose link assignment is unknown. "Channel" (warp, SM, QP) is the wrong unit; destination address is the right one. (Gap 6)
C6Causality crosses ranks
Barriers turn a remote delay into a local wait and erase who was last. Diagnosis needs a cross-rank timeline; on one node the clocks allow it cheaply, across nodes they do not, and the kernel is too short for per-kernel sync in decode. (Gaps 2, 8)
C7The communication kernel is not alone
It is designed to run beside GEMMs and beside its own sibling (combine). Profilers that serialise, counters that total the device, and replay-based tools are structurally unable to see the interaction. (Gaps 6, 10)
C8Two regimes, one kernel
Prefill is a bandwidth problem at 0.7 ms with 17 iterations per warp; decode is a latency chain at tens of µs with one iteration. The sampling budget, the acceptable probe cost and the value of clock sync differ by an order of magnitude; one tool must serve both. (Gap 8)
C9Shared memory is not free for probes
FabricPerf parks timestamps in shared memory because NCCL leaves it unused. DeepEP uses all 227 KB. Tracing must give up one dispatch warp's slot, live in registers, or stream to L2-resident global memory with care about perturbing the traffic it measures.
C10The library already knows what the hardware hides
DeepEP computes destination rank, byte count and region for every store before issuing it. A tracing hook at that point gives the address-classified accounting that a binary instrumenter would have to reconstruct. Cooperative instrumentation is cheaper and more exact than external probing here, and it is portable to NCCL's device API.

4.1 Practical constraints on this node

  • At the time of writing GPUs 1 to 3 are busy with another user's processes (100 % utilisation, ≈ 77 GB used each) and GPUs 4 to 7 are idle. Eight-GPU experiments must wait or be scheduled with that user; four-GPU runs on 4 to 7 are possible now (same CPU socket, same switch fabric). Per the machine rules, nothing on 1 to 3 is to be touched.
  • DeepEP is installed in the deepep conda env (see install-deepep.md); the traced kernels are JIT-compiled from headers, so the instrumentation patch needs no reinstall, only a cache flush.
  • bench_kineto flushes L2 and runs an all-reduce barrier between iterations; both must be disabled for E5 and E8, where the cache state and the overlap are the subject.
  • CUPTI PM sampling and NVML can run concurrently with the kernels; Nsight Compute cannot be used on the cooperative kernels at all.

4.2 The instrumentation patch

Everything above needs one mechanism: per-lane timestamps at a handful of points in dispatch_impl (and the mirror points in combine_impl), buffered and flushed without disturbing the traffic.

points
before tma_store_wait() at the top of the loop and after it (per lane; a lane that issued no store returns immediately, which is itself the "no destination" marker); before and after the slot atomicAdd; after mbarrier_wait (load latency); just before each lane's tma_store_1d (issue time, destination, bytes); SM 0: phase C done, D done, E issued, each F slot ready, H done; barrier arrive and release on both barriers; kernel entry and the line after the closing barrier.
clock
%globaltimer (32 ns granularity on this hardware per FabricPerf; fine for µs waits) for anything compared across GPUs; clock64 for atomic latency.
record
16 bytes: 40-bit timestamp, 8-bit event, 8-bit destination, 32-bit token index, 16-bit duration or bytes. Per warp per dispatch ≈ 17 tokens × (1 wait + 1 atomic + 1 load + ≤ 8 issues) ≈ 190 records ≈ 3 KB.
buffer
Shared memory is full. Option A: launch with 14 dispatch warps instead of 15 and use the freed 14,496 B slot as a per-SM trace ring (fits 900 records, enough for a dispatch at 4,096 tokens on 14 warps × 17 tokens if issue records are coalesced to one per token with a destination mask). Option B: registers for the current iteration's timestamps and one coalesced 32-byte store per token per warp to a global trace buffer with L2::evict_last: 4,096 × 32 B = 128 KB per GPU per dispatch, negligible against 300 MB, and no shared-memory cost. B is the default; A is the control that shows B does not perturb.
cost
Two globaltimer reads and one coalesced store per token per warp against a 40 µs iteration: well under 1 %. Validate by comparing E0 throughput with and without tracing.
cross-GPU time
Once per session: ping-pong between every GPU pair through the NCCL window, both directions, 1,000 rounds; offset = half-RTT corrected; report the asymmetry estimate from the two directions as the error bar rather than assuming symmetry. Re-check at the end of the session for drift.
analysis
Merge per-rank traces into one timeline; derive per-(src, dst) latency series, in-flight bytes series, warp stall series, per-rank phase timeline, and the critical path per dispatch. Plots: latency-versus-time per destination; in-flight bytes vs throughput; tail CDF per warp.

5. What a tool must provide, and where this differs from FabricPerf

Information that resolves the problems above, in order of how directly it can be had today.

  1. Per-(source, destination, region) issue accounting with per-destination completion latency, logged by the library at the point where it already knows all three. Available now with the patch; the gap is only that "completion" needs calibration (E1). This is the memory-semantic completion queue.
  2. A cross-rank in-kernel timeline with a one-time clock calibration per node and explicit asymmetry error bars, plus clock-free arrival ordering at barriers via fetch-add. Available now on one node; needs the GIN path's signals, timestamped on arrival, across nodes.
  3. Window state: in-flight bytes per destination (derived from 1) and the hardware's outstanding-store limit (E1). Turns "nothing is busy" into "the window is closed".
  4. Address-to-path attribution: window offset → L2 slice and NVLink link, recovered by sweeps against per-link counters. Needed to reason about P3 and P4 instead of trial and error.
  5. Receiver ingress per source and per-link queue occupancy at µs resolution: hardware only. NVSwitch port counters and NVLink TX credit stall counters exist in the silicon; exposing them per kernel, or readable from a device kernel, is the request to make of the vendor and the thing an open fabric (UALink, SUE) could specify.
  6. Per-SM-set attribution of shared memory-system counters, so that a communication kernel and a GEMM sharing a GPU can each be charged for their L2 and HBM use. Hardware again; the software substitute is 1 plus time-correlation.

Relation to FabricPerf. FabricPerf measures a protocol that has a fence, a flag and a poll per channel step, aligns two clocks per kernel, and models the fabric as memory traffic. DeepEP has none of the three landmarks on its data plane, overlaps with compute by design, and runs in a regime (decode) where per-kernel clock sync costs more than the kernel. The programme above therefore inverts the design: one-clock sender-side completion as the primary signal, calibrated once; cross-rank time as a secondary, session-calibrated signal; address rather than channel as the unit of attribution; and cooperative instrumentation inside the communication library rather than binary probes around it. FabricPerf's two findings, channel imbalance and LLC eviction, reappear here as P5 and P6, but each with a cause the experiments can name.

Not this: adding fences and flags to DeepEP's data plane so that T1 to T5 can be attached. That measures a different, slower protocol.

Research questions this programme would answer. (1) Does NVLink/NVSwitch exhibit victim-flow head-of-line blocking under destination incast, and how much of DeepEP's incast penalty is the fabric versus the one-token window? (2) What is the completion semantics and the outstanding-store credit limit of bulk asynchronous stores to peer memory, and can their wait time serve as a calibrated congestion signal? (3) How much of overlapped MoE time is lost to compute-communication interference, on which side, through which resource? (4) In the decode regime, which µs belong to synchronisation, to address-serialised atomics, to control-plane queueing and to the host, and does fixing the layout move them?
